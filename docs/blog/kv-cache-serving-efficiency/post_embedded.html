The KV Cache-Miss Tax: Quantifying Efficiency in LLM Inference Serving

Kaushik Mitra · Draft v0.7

TL;DR

Two serving stacks on the same GPUs and the same workload can deliver very different throughput. This post asks two questions of each stack. How close does it get to what the hardware allows? Where does the rest go? One ratio answers both.

The ratio has four inputs: the workload's intrinsic prefix overlap, its mean input and output lengths, the model's shape, and five hardware numbers. One of the five, the prefill rate, is calibrated on the deployment with a short job. The rest come from the datasheet and the engine's startup log, and a second short job checks that the engine reaches the datasheet bandwidth (Appendix A). From these inputs we compute a ceiling: the most throughput, input plus output tokens per second, the hardware can sustain on that workload. At each load level, the steady-state throughput the system served, divided by the ceiling, is its serving efficiency η. The ceiling is specific to the workload, so η compares systems on one workload. It is not a hardware utilisation figure.

The shortfall from the ceiling splits into four factors whose product is η:

  1. Client: how much of the ceiling the load asked for. This is the throughput an engine that loses nothing would deliver to these sessions, given their idle time between turns.

  2. Reuse: prompt tokens the system had to recompute, either because the request went to a replica that did not hold their KV or because the KV had been evicted.

  3. Engine: what this engine version costs beyond an ideal one at a steady batch, measured on one replica: a fixed cost per step, and prefill chunks that stall the decodes they ride with.

  4. Orchestration: everything else lost at the hit rate the run had, in the layers around the engine: uneven load across replicas, turns arriving in waves, queueing, tier copies in the critical path.

We ran one agentic coding workload on an NVIDIA B200 fleet with a host KV tier, with each stack in its documented default configuration. llm-d reached an η of 0.29 and round-robin 0.09. These numbers describe this workload, fleet and configuration. They are not a general ranking of the stacks. The prefix hit rate each stack kept explains much of the gap between them. llm-d holds its hit rate at 0.90 or above until HBM fills with the sessions it is decoding, at 240 sessions. Round-robin is already down to 0.62 at 90 sessions, with HBM a quarter full. The framework is for infrastructure engineers building or selecting serving stacks, and for vendors who want standardised performance claims.

1. The question

The GPUs are no faster for one stack than for another. What differs is how much work each stack repeats and how much of the hardware it leaves idle. Every request brings a prompt. When the stack can reuse that prompt's KV cache from an earlier turn, it skips recomputing those tokens. When it cannot, it spends GPU time on work it could have avoided.

Reuse is not the whole story. A stack can keep almost every reusable token and still lose throughput to memory pressure, preemption or slow steps. Telling these losses apart needs a yardstick that does not move with the stack.

That yardstick is serving efficiency η: achieved throughput divided by the workload-specific hardware ceiling. The post makes two contributions. The first is a metric: η and a way to compute its ceiling. The second is a diagnosis: the split of the shortfall, read alongside the engine's counters, says whether the client, routing, retention, capacity or the engine is responsible. The second is the one an operator uses day to day. The B200 results of Section 6 demonstrate both.

In this framework, whatever a mechanism costs or saves in communication, scheduling or placement shows up in one of four places: the reuse factor, the engine factor, the orchestration factor, or the number of sessions the fleet can hold.

The centrality of the KV cache

Every token a transformer generates is computed from the key and value (KV) tensors of every preceding token. This KV cache is the only large state an inference system carries across tokens, conversation turns and prefix-sharing requests. Model weights are static and shared. Activations are transient. So the costs a stack can shift from one request to the next are mostly KV decisions: compute it or fetch it, where to keep it and for how long, which block to evict, and which replica the request should land on so that request and KV meet.

The industry has built specialised solutions at three layers of the stack:

  • Routers. Prefix-cache-aware routing sends a request to the replica that holds its KV.

  • Pool topologies. Prefill/decode disaggregation runs the two phases on different pools and ships the KV once.

  • Storage layers. Tiered offload moves KV below the accelerator instead of dropping it. Peer-to-peer sharing pulls it from a neighbour instead of recomputing it.

Seen from the KV cache, these are facets of one placement problem.

The workload

In this post a workload is a trace of conversations. Each turn brings a prompt, receives an output, and pauses before the next. The framework reduces the trace to three numbers: the mean prompt length I, the mean output length O, and the intrinsic prefix overlap ρ, the share of prompt tokens a cache of unlimited size would find already computed. The turn count and the pause enter through the client term of Section 4. The post uses one workload throughout: the agentic coding trace of the llm-d agentic-serving guide, the workload behind the llm-d blog post Serving GLM-5.2 for Agentic Workloads on llm-d, replayed with inference-perf:

2. Compute or memory: where a run sits on the roofline

An engine serves requests in steps. One step is one forward pass over everything scheduled in it: a chunk of prefill tokens from prompts still being processed, plus one new token for every request that is decoding. A step takes the longer of its compute time and its memory time. This post calls that the step-time bound: a roofline applied to one step. The next paragraph says why a step waits on one or the other.

In every step a GPU does two things. It reads bytes from HBM, and it does arithmetic on them. A decode step for a long context reads a lot: the weights and every resident context's KV, hundreds of gigabytes. It computes little, one token per request. It waits on memory. A prefill step computes a lot per byte it reads. It waits on compute. The roofline chart is the standard picture of which of the two a step waited on. The rest of this section applies it to a serving fleet, where the hit rate decides how much prefill each step carries.

A roofline chart places a run by two numbers. The x axis is its arithmetic intensity: the FLOPs it performed per byte read from HBM. The y axis is the FLOP/s it achieved. The hardware caps the run from two sides. The compute roof is flat, at the peak FLOP/s. The memory roof slopes. A step that does few FLOPs per byte it reads is limited by HBM, and the most FLOP/s it can reach is the bandwidth times its arithmetic intensity. That limit rises with arithmetic intensity until it meets the compute roof. The two roofs meet at the ridge. Left of the ridge, a step spends longer reading memory than computing. Right of it, longer computing.

The figure places the six fleet runs of Section 6 on this chart. The dashed roof is the B200 datasheet. The solid roof is this deployment's. Its compute roof is the prefill rate calibrated on one replica. Its memory roof is the datasheet bandwidth, which a steady decode batch of eight or more reaches (Appendix A). The two roofs meet at about 150 FLOP/B.

The hit rate decides where a run lands. At a 0.98 hit rate the fleet sits on the memory roof, because nearly all of its steps are decode steps. As the hit rate falls, the same workload moves right and up, across the ridge and onto the compute roof. The GPUs get busier and the useful token rate gets lower. The added compute is recomputing contexts the stack failed to reuse.

Applied to a workload, compute-bound and memory-bound say which resource limits its ceiling, the throughput at hit rate ρ (Section 3). Applied to a run, they say which term dominated its steps: left of the ridge memory, right of it compute. Section 2.1 gives the rule for which side a workload or a run falls on. The hit rate enters the rule only through U, the uncached input per turn. The workload's intrinsic overlap ρ places the ceiling. A run's measured hit rate h places that run. A run's side can also be read from its arithmetic intensity, computed from the engine's token counters and the model's per-token costs, which is how the chart above places it. Appendix A lists every symbol in one table.

2.1 Which side of the ridge a workload falls on

Each output token costs the engine two things. One is a read of its whole context, L tokens of KV. The other is the compute for its share of the uncached prompt, U/O prefill tokens. The ridge of Section 2, written in token units as r, is the exchange rate between them: the number of KV tokens the device reads in the time it prefills one token. It comes from the two calibrated rates:

r = (HBM bandwidth / KV bytes per token) / prefill tokens per second

r depends on the hardware, the model and the context at which the prefill rate is calibrated. It does not depend on the hit rate or the load. For Qwen3-Coder-480B on 8 × B200, with the prefill rate calibrated at this workload's 117k context, r is about 11,000.

Compute dominates a step when the compute per output token exceeds the memory read per output token, with both counted in prefill-token time. One correction accounts for the weights a step streams. They count as extra context shared by the n sequences in the step: W_b(n)/(n·b) tokens, where b is the KV bytes per token. For a mixture-of-experts model W_b depends on n. Appendix B derives it. The rule is

U/O > (L + W_b(n)/(n·b)) / r − 1

For the agentic workload (L = 117k, U = 7.3k, O = 386), U/O is 19. The threshold depends on the batch, because the weight read is shared across it: about 24 at a batch of 12 and about 15 at the capacity cap of 29 sequences, derived in Appendix B.1. Memory dominates up to a batch of about 20 and compute above it. The ceiling is reached at a batch of 20 or more (Section 3), so at its ceiling the workload is compute-bound, by a margin of 10 to 15%. Capacity is not in the rule. It caps n at 29 and enters the ceiling as a separate condition.

3. The ceiling and η

A step, as defined in Section 2, incurs three hardware costs:

  1. Compute. Every token costs the model's active FLOPs plus attention over its request's context. A prefill chunk is almost all of it.

  2. Bandwidth. Every step streams the weights it touches and reads the KV of every decoding request. Its memory time grows with the number and length of resident contexts.

  3. Capacity. Every request whose KV is held, decoding or waiting for its next turn, occupies HBM. When HBM is full something is evicted. Unless a lower tier still holds it, it must be recomputed when it returns.

Serving efficiency η is one number per run. It is the steady-state tokens per second per accelerator a system achieved at one offered load, divided by the ceiling R. R is the tokens per second the same accelerators could sustain under two ideal conditions: every reusable prefix token is reused (h = ρ), and KV occupies HBM only while its request is decoding. Tokens are input plus output. A cached prompt token counts as served just as a recomputed one does. What differs is what it cost.

How much a system serves depends on load. A stack's headline η is therefore the highest over a sweep of offered loads, and the runs below the peak are reported beside it.

η = achieved tokens/s per accelerator / R

R comes from the step-time bound. A replica's throughput is the most it can push through while every step stays within compute, bandwidth and HBM capacity (equations in Appendix B).

R is an upper bound by construction. Its definition carries two assumptions. The hit rate is ρ, so any reuse the system fails to find, keep or share shows up as a shortfall. Paused sessions' KV costs nothing while it is out of HBM. Two further assumptions describe an ideal engine: prefill is spread evenly over the steps rather than run in whole chunks, and a step's compute and memory time overlap. Appendix A measures what this engine does instead, and Section 4 charges the difference to it. Smaller omissions are listed in Section 8.

The ceiling for the B200 fleet

On the B200 deployment calibrated in Appendix A, the agentic workload (I = 117k, O = 386, ρ = 0.94) has a ceiling of 44.5k tokens per second per GPU, or 2.14M for the six-replica fleet. It is compute-bound. The figure draws the throughput the step-time bound allows as the number of sequences decoding per replica grows. Up to 20 sequences the memory time sets the step and the line rises. From 20 on, the compute for the prefill that even an oracle cannot avoid sets the step: the first turn of each session and the new tokens of every turn. That compute grows in proportion to the batch, so the line goes flat. Adding sessions beyond 20 adds nothing. HBM holds 29 decoding sequences, so the batch cannot grow past that in any case. The margin is thin. From 20 to 29 the memory time is 85 to 90% of the compute time, inside the calibration's uncertainty. The two resources are nearly balanced at the ceiling, with compute slightly ahead. The line assumes HBM holds decoding sequences only.

At the ceiling, about 36 sessions per replica are paused in a tool call at any moment: three turns a second times a 12 s pause. Their KV has to live somewhere else. A fleet whose HBM also held them would stop at 7 sequences and 0.55 of R. The host tier is what moves that wall.

4. Where the shortfall goes

η judges a system against the hardware. A measured run also reflects its load generator. The decomposition therefore treats the generator as a client whose behaviour is given. It uses only what the client controls and what the system did with it.

In a closed loop the client controls two things. One is the number of sessions S it runs. The other is Z, how long each session sits idle between the server finishing a turn and the client sending the next. That is tool time plus anything else the client does. Neither needs the server to measure.

Four per-turn service times enter. T is the measured time per turn. T_ideal is what an engine that loses nothing would take over the same steady window, at the oracle's hit rate of 0.98: O decode steps, each at the step-time bound's duration for the run's batch. It is not zero, since output comes one token per step. T_h is the same quantity at the hit rate h the run achieved. T_eng is the turn time of this engine at a steady batch and that hit rate, from the single-replica ladder of Appendix A: the measured step at the run's batch, plus one measured chunk step for every 8k tokens of prefill the hit rate leaves.

An engine that loses nothing would see each session cycle in Z + T_ideal. The most this client could get from any server is therefore S(I + O)/(Z + T_ideal). Little's law then gives η as a product of four factors:

  • Client, S(I + O)/(Z + T_ideal) over R. How much of the ceiling the client asked for: what its sessions and idle time would get from an engine that loses nothing, at the batch those sessions produce. This is mostly a property of the load generator. The one way the system reaches in is through the batch: T_ideal is evaluated at the run's own batch, and a slower engine holds more requests decoding at once. The factor is not capped. It exceeds 1 when the client offers more than the ceiling can serve. The product then still equals η, and the wait that even an engine at the ceiling would impose lands in the orchestration factor. No run in this post reaches 1. The largest is 0.57: even at 300 sessions the harness offered the fleet about half of what the ceiling could serve.

  • Reuse, (Z + T_ideal)/(Z + T_h). Time lost recomputing prompt tokens, either because the request went to a replica that did not hold their KV or because the KV had been evicted. It measures the effect of every decision about where KV is, routing, admission and retention alike. Section 7 separates the causes.

  • Engine, (Z + T_h)/(Z + T_eng). What this engine version costs against the ideal one, at the hit rate the run had: a fixed cost per step, and prefill chunks that stall the decodes they ride with. It is measured, not modelled.

  • Orchestration, (Z + T_eng)/(Z + T). Everything else lost at that hit rate: the difference between a steady single replica and the fleet. Its known contents are uneven load across replicas, from the router or the admission gate; turns arriving in waves, so the batch swings; waits in the queue and after preemption; and host-tier copies in the critical path. It is a residual, so it also holds whatever the ladder did not measure.

The ideal uses 0.98 rather than ρ because of how the replay runs. It starts every conversation at once, so the first turns, whose whole prompt is new, fall outside the steady window. A live service would see them at ρ's rate, one turn in twenty. The ceiling therefore keeps ρ. The runs' hit rates were measured in the window, so the ideal they are held to is the oracle over that window. Held to ρ instead, llm-d at 120 sessions, with h = 0.98, would beat the ideal. The choice does not touch R, which keeps ρ. It would matter if it did. With compute binding, R scales with the tokens served per token the oracle still prefills, and a ceiling at 0.98 would be 2.8 times the one at ρ.

Two runs of Section 6 show how the factors read. llm-d at 120 sessions: client 0.35, reuse 1.00, engine 0.93, orchestration 0.88; η 0.28. The client asked for a third of the ceiling. The stack recomputed nothing. The engine lost 7% of what was asked and the orchestration a further 12%. Round-robin at 90 sessions: client 0.20, reuse 0.70, engine 0.87, orchestration 0.73; η 0.09. The client asked for a fifth of the ceiling. Recompute took 30% of that. The engine lost a further 13% and the orchestration 27%.

The factors multiply to η exactly. The client term is S(I + O)/(Z + T_ideal) over R, and the other three telescope from Z + T_ideal to Z + T. For the six runs the product matches the measured η to within 0.03%. The residual is rounding in the recorded throughput.

The factors are not independent causes. Each is a ratio of cycle times under one chosen ideal engine. The split is an attribution under that model, not a unique causal decomposition. A loss can move between reuse and the engine and orchestration factors where the idealisation misses. The note on small batches below gives the mechanism.

Reading down a stack's rows in the figure, more sessions lengthen the bar. What the added length turns into is the diagnosis. For llm-d it is throughput until HBM fills, then engine and orchestration loss. For round-robin it is recompute from the first step. The extra sessions are recomputed rather than served. That is a routing limit, not a load limit.

The engine factor is measured, not modelled. Appendix A's ladder gives this vLLM version's step at each batch with no prefill in flight, and its step when a full 8k prefill chunk rides along. Two costs separate it from the ideal engine. One is a fixed cost of about 7 ms per step, which at small batches is most of the step: at a batch of one the ideal step is 2 ms and the measured one 7.5 ms. The other is chunk steps that do not overlap with decode steps: at 116k context a step carrying an 8k chunk takes 364 ms, its full prefill time, while the decodes in it wait. Both are properties of the engine version, not of the stack around it. They cost 7 to 21% in the runs of Section 6.

The orchestration factor is a residual. It holds what the layers around the engine did with the hit rate they had: how turns were admitted and batched, when tier copies ran, how long requests waited. Its largest named content is the batch swing. T_eng is computed at the run's mean batch. Throughput per step grows with the batch, but more slowly as the batch gets larger, so a batch that swings above and below its mean delivers less than a steady batch at that mean. For llm-d at 180 and 240 sessions that difference is about 6% of the ideal throughput. The orchestration factor there is 0.73 and 0.70, so the swing is about a fifth of the loss. The rest is host-tier copies in the critical path and requests waiting for KV blocks, which the counters of Section 6 show rising with the sessions. These are costs of turns arriving in waves, which an admission gate could smooth, not faults of the engine.

One artefact of the ideal remains, at small batches. The step-time bound spreads a turn's prefill evenly across its decode steps. At a small batch that spread-out prefill fits under the step's memory time, so the ideal engine charges little for it. Recompute that the real engine did pay for then shows up in the engine and orchestration factors rather than in reuse. Round-robin at 90 sessions shows it. Its hit rate is 0.62, below llm-d at 300 sessions, yet its reuse factor is 0.70 against 0.57, and engine times orchestration 0.64. Low engine and orchestration factors at a small batch should therefore be read with the hit rate beside it. Section 7 has the row.

Retention is not one of the factors. It sets where the peak falls: the number of sessions the system can hold before it starts evicting. It is reported beside η as a capacity.

5. How a stack keeps the hit rate up

where KV can be and how it moves · one index, three moves

A turn's prompt KV is in one of three places. It is on the replica that will serve it. It is somewhere it can be fetched from. Or it is nowhere, in which case it is recomputed.

A stack has three moves to make the first case the common one. It can route the request to the replica that holds its prefix. It can move the KV: to a host tier when its session pauses, or from a peer when it resumes elsewhere. It can move the compute, running prefill on a separate pool and shipping the KV once.

Each move has a price, in transfer time or in load concentrated on one replica. A router's job is to pay it only when it beats recompute. Eviction is not a move. It is what happens when every tier is full, and the evicted KV's next use pays a full recompute. The two stacks of Section 6 differ in the first move. llm-d routes each turn toward the replica its index believes holds the prefix. Round-robin keeps no index and routes blind. Both move KV to the host tier when a session pauses, and neither moves compute.

6. Empirical evaluation: two stacks on a B200 fleet

Setup

Two stacks were evaluated on one six-replica NVIDIA B200 fleet running the agentic coding workload of the llm-d agentic-serving guide. The model is Qwen3-Coder-480B-A35B-FP8 at TP=8 with expert parallelism, on vLLM v0.28.0. Both stacks used the same six nodes, engine flags, KV allocation (3,403,712 tokens in HBM per replica), trace and seeds. Every replica also had a 1.61 TB host KV tier (vLLM's OffloadingConnector, write-through, 6.19M tokens). The tier was registered with the GPUs in 64 GiB pieces through a backport of vLLM PR #51081, because a single registration of a tier above 512 GiB fails on this platform.

  1. llm-d, in the default configuration of its agentic-serving guide: the approximate prefix index (hashed prompt prefixes scored against each replica's recent history), a prefill-rate affinity filter calibrated to the measured 37,217 tokens per second, and the token-load scorer. The index was set to 193,500 blocks per replica. Its tokenizer-free estimate counts 4 bytes as one token, which is about 1.5 real tokens for this code-heavy trace. That setting covers about 128k engine blocks: more than the host tier alone, less than both tiers together. Its precise index (KV events from each engine) was not used.

  2. Round-robin, a plain Kubernetes Service, as the baseline.

Each stack ran an ascending ladder of session counts until its throughput had fallen: llm-d at 120, 180, 240 and 300 sessions and round-robin at 90 and 120. Throughput is steady-state. η is taken at each stack's peak over its ladder (Section 3). The engines' counters were sampled every 15 s: uncached prefill tokens, tokens restored from the host tier, generated tokens, steps, resident and waiting requests, KV occupancy and preemptions. Achieved FLOP/s, HBM traffic and arithmetic intensity follow from them and the model shape. Every level ran on all six replicas, checked from each replica's own counters.

Key takeaways

  • The knee is HBM capacity for the sessions being decoded. llm-d keeps the hit rate at the workload's level up to 240 sessions, with the host tier supplying up to two thirds of the prompt tokens. Past 180 sessions, the KV of the requests decoding at once fills HBM and the engine starts preempting. HBM used in the tables is the mean of the engine's KV-block gauge over the window, and the gauge swings. At 240 sessions the mean is 65%, but the 95th percentile on every replica is 96% or more and the peak is 100%. The preemptions fall at those peaks. A preempted request is restored from the host tier rather than recomputed. But it waits for a slot and for the copy, so turns get longer and throughput falls while the hit rate holds.

  • The hit rate explains much of the throughput gap in these runs. Round-robin kept 0.62 to 0.53, against the 0.98 an oracle reaches over the same window. It peaks with HBM about a quarter full and no preemptions. Its shortfall starts with routing: a turn lands on a replica that holds none of its context in either tier, and the prompt is recomputed. No stack was tuned beyond llm-d's documented prefill-rate calibration.

  • The rule and the roofline agree. The rule of Section 2.1, applied to each run's measured hit rate, context and batch size, puts all six runs on the same side of the ridge (about 150 FLOP/B) as their arithmetic intensity. llm-d at 120 sessions, with U/O of 6 against a threshold of 37 at its batch of six, is memory-bound by both. The other five are compute-bound by both. The closest call is llm-d at 180 sessions: U/O of 28 against a threshold of 26, at 172 FLOP/B. Both use the same per-token costs. This is a consistency check, not an independent measurement.

inference-perf's conversation replay (v0.7.0) advances conversations in lockstep when used as documented. 9. Conclusion

The roofline framework reduces serving evaluation to one ratio, η. It splits the shortfall into four factors that each name a lever: the load offered, reuse, the engine, and the orchestration around it.

Three claims in this post have different standing. Observed: on the B200 fleet measured here, llm-d reached 0.29 and round-robin 0.09. Explained: the factors attribute much of that gap to the prefix hit rate each stack kept, under the documented configurations. Proposed: the same procedure diagnoses other workloads and deployments. The first two rest on the runs of Section 6. The third rests on applying it elsewhere. The reproduction kit is there for that.

Appendix B. The step-time bound and the ridge

This appendix derives the ceiling R used throughout from the three costs of a step described in Section 3. It uses the symbols of Sections 2 and 3 and the calibrated inputs of Appendix A.

B.1 The step-time bound

Engines run continuous batching with chunked prefill. One scheduler iteration holds up to T_b tokens of prefill chunks plus one token for every sequence in decode, and runs them as one forward pass. Prefill and decode are not two pipelines with separate bounds. They share one iteration, and a prefill chunk lengthens the step for every decode sequence in it. The iteration is therefore the unit.

With n_p prefill tokens and n_d decode tokens in the iteration, its duration is the larger of its compute time and its memory time. Little's law ties the counts to the request rate λ per replica at hit rate h:

t_iter = max( 2N(n_p + n_d)/F , (W_b + n_d·b·c̄ + (n_p/T_b)·b·I)/W ), n_p = λ·I·(1−h)·t_iter, n_d = λ·O·t_iter, c̄ = I + O/2

The memory term reads the weights once, the KV of every decode sequence once (each decode token attends over its whole context), and the prefix once per prefill chunk. c̄ is the time-average resident context. A request's KV traces a trapezoid in memory and time, from I at arrival to I + O at exit.

A rate is sustainable only while all four of the following hold. The first to fail as λ rises is the binding resource:

compute: 2Nλ(I(1−h) + O) < F · bandwidth: λ·b·(O·c̄ + I(1−h)·I/T_b) < W · capacity: n_d·c̄ + (n_p/T_b)·I/2 ≤ C · budget: n_p ≤ T_b

Each condition on its own limits λ, the request rate one replica sustains. Three of the four have a closed form. The prefill-budget condition never binds at an 8k chunk. With U = (1 − ρ)·I and L = I + O/2, and dividing the replica's token rate by the accelerators it spans (8 GPUs at TP=8) to get the per-accelerator R:

λ_c = F / (2N(U + O)), λ_w = W / (b(OL + UI/T_b)), λ_m = C / ( (W_b/W)(OL + UI/(2T_b)) + C·b(OL + UI/T_b)/W ), R = (I + O)·min(λ_c, λ_w, λ_m) / accelerators per replica

The closed forms and the kit agree, but they are not the same computation. ceiling_v3.py enumerates the batch n, applies the step-time bound at each n, and stops at the largest integer n with n · c̄ ≤ C, counting decoding KV only. At the B200 point the binding form is λ_c: 3.04 requests per second per replica, and the enumeration gives the same to three figures from a batch of 20 up. Written out, R per replica is F(I) · (I + O)/(O + U): the calibrated prefill rate at the workload's context times the tokens served per token the oracle still has to prefill, 23,260 × 117,070 / 7,620 = 357k tokens per second, or 44.5k per GPU. λ_w is 5.6, and λ_m would bind only below a batch of 20, where the enumeration follows the memory term. The bandwidth constraint and λ_w leave out the weight stream W_b. They are the large-batch limit of the bound. The kit keeps W_b in every step. A reader implementing the closed forms should expect agreement with the kit to within about 0.2% at this workload, and should use the enumeration where the integer batch or the weight stream matters.

For a dense model that is the whole computation. For a mixture-of-experts model W_b depends on how many tokens share a step. It is therefore evaluated at the tokens the solution implies: the n = λ_m·O·t_iter decoding tokens plus the n·U/O prefill tokens spread into the step. The pair converges in two or three iterations.

For the B200 fleet the ceiling is reached at 20 resident sequences per replica and holds to the capacity cap of 29. The step at 29 looks like this. It carries 29 decode tokens and about 545 prefill tokens: the uncached 7k tokens of a turn spread over that turn's 386 decode steps, summed over the 29 sequences. Its memory time is the weights those 574 tokens touch, nearly all 480 GB of experts, plus the KV of 29 contexts of 117k tokens, about 860 GB. That is 1.34 TB at the datasheet 64 TB/s per replica, or 21 ms. Its compute time is 574 tokens at the calibrated 23k prefill tokens per second, or 25 ms. Compute sets the step and memory runs at 85%. Both terms grow in proportion to n from 20 on, so throughput is flat there: λ_c = 3.04 requests per second per replica, and the 44.5k tokens per second per GPU used throughout. Below 20 the memory term is the longer one and the line of Section 3 is still rising. The batch cannot pass 29 because 29 contexts plus their half-finished outputs fill the 3.4M-token KV allocation. The cap is C divided by I + O/2: 3,403,712 over 116,877 tokens per sequence, or 29.1. The ceiling is compute-bound by a margin of 10 to 15%, and capacity caps the batch without setting R.

Two corrections apply. With a distribution of output lengths the resident context is I + ½Ō(1 + CV²), because a snapshot of the batch catches long generations in proportion to their length. At CV² = 1 the decode share doubles. Writing the iteration as a sum instead of a max is the conservative form that bounds an engine overlapping nothing. Measured inter-token latencies in the llm-d guides sit nearer the max. W_b for a mixture of experts is the dense weights plus the experts the step's tokens touch, decode and prefill alike. With E experts and k active per token, that is E(1 − (1 − k/E)^n)·e expert weights: from about 35 GB at one token to 374 GB at 29 and nearly all 480 GB once a step carries a few hundred tokens, for Qwen3-Coder-480B. The ceilings used here take that form.

B.2 The ridge

The ridge is the point where the two measured roofs of Section 2 meet. In FLOP per byte it is the compute roof divided by the memory roof, F/W, about 150 on this deployment. Section 2.1 states the same crossing in token units, r = (W/b)/R_pf: the number of KV tokens the memory system reads in the time the compute prefills one token. At this workload's 117k context, r is about 11,000.

Both roofs use one FLOP convention. A token's FLOPs are 2N for the linear layers plus its attention over the context, 4 · layers · query heads · head dim · L. At 117k context that is 4.3 × 10¹¹ FLOPs per token, about six times 2N. The compute roof is the calibrated prefill rate at 128k, 22,794 tokens per second for the eight-GPU replica, times that count: 9.8 PFLOP/s for the replica, which is 1.22 PFLOP/s per GPU. The arithmetic intensity of each run in the chart is computed with the same count. The step-time bound of B.1 does not count attention FLOPs explicitly. It keeps the 2N convention and lets the calibrated prefill rate fall with context instead. Its F is therefore α_F F_peak (I/8192)^p, and that is why Appendix A quotes α_F as 0.15 of the bf16 peak at 8k. The two conventions give the same ridge: 150 FLOP/B × b × r = 4.3 × 10¹¹ FLOPs per token.

Every quantity in this appendix is per replica of eight GPUs unless it says per GPU. b = 253,952 bytes is the KV of one token across the whole model: 2 · 62 layers · 8 KV heads · 128 · 2 bytes. At TP=8 each GPU holds one of the eight KV heads, 31,744 bytes per token, with no replication. W = 8 × 8 TB/s = 64 TB/s is the datasheet bandwidth of the eight GPUs together, which the ladder of Appendix A shows the engine reaching from a batch of eight. C = 3,403,712 tokens is the KV capacity the engine reports for the replica. Each GPU holds its 31,744-byte shard of every one of those tokens, so C · b is 805 GiB across the replica, about 101 GiB per GPU. With these values, r = (64 × 10¹² / 253,952) / 22,794 = 252 × 10⁶ / 22,794 ≈ 11,060. Dividing both W and b by eight leaves r unchanged. The FLOP/B ridge is likewise the same per GPU and per replica: 1.22 PFLOP/s over 8 TB/s equals 9.76 PFLOP/s over 64 TB/s, about 150 either way.

The rule of Section 2.1 follows from r. Per output token, a request costs 1 + U/O prefill tokens of compute and L tokens of KV read. Compute dominates when 1 + U/O exceeds L/r. The weight stream enters as a shared read. A step with n sequences reads W_b(n) bytes of weights once, which is W_b(n)/(n·b) tokens' worth of KV per sequence, so L becomes L + W_b(n)/(n·b). Section 2.1 draws that threshold for several n. Because the prefill rate falls with context, r grows with L. At the 8k calibration context r would be about 6,800. That raises the agentic threshold at a batch of 29 from 15 to about 26, above the workload's 19, and would call the ceiling memory-bound. Which side the ceiling falls on therefore depends on calibrating the prefill rate at the workload's own context, which is why Appendix A does.

Two limits follow. With nothing cached, U = L = I. A long prompt is then memory-bound once the output exceeds about r tokens, 11,000 here. With everything cached, U = 0. Any reused context longer than r is then memory-bound at any output length. The agentic workload sits between the two. Its oracle still prefills 7k tokens a turn, and at the ceiling's batch that compute slightly exceeds the read of its 117k context.
